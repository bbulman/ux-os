Build one by interview

One command interviews you and writes taste.md. The interview works the way /grill-me does: it settles every decision the file rests on, one round of questions at a time, until nothing is left assumed. Run it after DESIGN.md, which holds the rules it must not repeat. The owner answers.

/ux-os:create-taste-file

It reads first

DESIGN.md, any critiques, review notes or design-loop learnings the project keeps, and the file’s own dated notes.

Then it asks about

  1. The root: what good means here, in two to five words
  2. Reference anchors to reason toward and away from, each with its specific quality
  3. The cut test: how a feature, section or element earns its place
  4. Soul: the kind of delight allowed, and how much
  5. What reads as machine-made
  6. The kill list
  7. Taste that is not settled yet

How it runs

  1. It looks up every fact it can before asking anything, and in a file that already exists it tests each claim against the code.
  2. It asks in rounds, and each question comes open: it offers its own draft only after you answer or say you have no view, because this file has to hold what you believe, not what it guessed.
  3. It shows a summary of the new file, section by section, and writes nothing until you say yes.
  4. A missing file, or one still the unfilled template, is written at the project root. A file that already exists is never changed: the new copy goes to .scratch/project-files/taste.md, with the facts it corrected and any citations into the old file that would break, for you to compare.

taste.md (Workshopr example)

An excerpt from Workshopr's own taste.md, trimmed to show the shape. Sections left out are marked.

Current. This file holds Bill's design taste: the judgment no check can enforce. DESIGN.md holds the rules and the system. DESIGN.md says what's allowed. This file says what's good.

A screen can pass every rule in DESIGN.md and every check the design-system enforcer runs, and still fail here. That's the gap this file exists for.

Lines in quotation marks are Bill's own words. The rest is paraphrased from his answers in a 100-question design taste interview (5 Aug 2026, design answers only) and from his notes on the agents in October 2026.

The root: beautiful simplicity

Offered a long theory of where his taste comes from, Bill cut it down to two words: "beautiful simplicity."

  • Useful, usable and beautiful. Most products nail one or two. All three are required.
  • Beauty is fit to the user. "If I design something that makes sense to them, that's where beauty and structure connect."
  • The test for beautiful: "I keep coming back to it because I want to."
  • The taste is structural, not decorative. The page's intent, white space, one proper call to action, and how the pages connect matter more than surface style.

Reference anchors

Reason toward:

  • Notion: complex underneath, feels simple.
  • Linear: beautiful because it's opinionated and makes decisions for you.
  • The original Evernote, before feature creep.
  • Charles and Ray Eames, Powers of Ten: design the layers, not only the surface.
  • Nicholas Felton's annual reports: data can be beautiful if it's done well.
  • Edward Tufte: information design.
  • BJ Fogg: persuasion, used honestly.

Reason away from:

  • Excel: powerful, useful to millions, and a kitchen sink. "Horrible to use."

Where the taste sits now: big-type minimalism, generous white space, and the actions right in front of you. Not only big sans serifs: better type, including a serif at the right moment. (The type pairing itself is DESIGN.md §7.)

What reads as "AI"

  • AI must be invisible. The moment you can see its fingerprints, it's slop. "AI-enhanced, not AI-driven": start with AI, then polish until it's yours.
  • An AI tell is the worst outcome. Bill would rather replace anything that reads as machine-made than keep it.
  • Visual tells: purple-blue gradient backgrounds (the default AI look) and decorative gradients. (Stock-photo hero images are already banned in DESIGN.md §14.3.)
  • "AI-powered" stamped on everything, and hype words in general (DESIGN.md §5 bans the specific ones). "I don't need AI in my toaster."
  • Functionally correct, and a bit off. A new screen can follow every rule and still "feel a bit off and a bit 'AI'". No check catches that yet. Name what's off in words, and if it can become a rule, put the rule in DESIGN.md.
  • The writing tells: everything in threes and inflated vocabulary are covered in DESIGN.md §5 and §6. Gratuitous bold is one Bill notices too, and DESIGN.md doesn't cover it yet.

The kill list

  • Decoration pretending to be design: decorative gradients, stock imagery used as a hero (DESIGN.md §14.3).
  • The everything-dashboard with endless toggles, and the 200-option settings page.
  • The database dump: everything thrown on the page at once.
  • An unclear call to action. People should know instantly what you want them to do next: one proper call to action, driving them from page to page.
  • Ignored page-to-page transitions. They're a design surface too.
  • Empty states with no guidance. They fail at the moment the user needs direction. First runs should get people to the moment they understand what the product is for, fast.
  • Modals. Bill hates them and relies on them anyway. That's an open tension, not a rule.
  • Inconsistency. It reads as a mess. Predictability lowers the load on the user.
